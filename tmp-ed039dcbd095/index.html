1. Two states say Grok's safety promises weren't true ⚖️

What happened: On Monday, the attorneys general (each state's top lawyer) of Oregon and Virginia filed separate but similar lawsuits against SpaceX, Elon Musk's company that now runs the X social network and the Grok chatbot. (xAI, the company that made Grok, merged into SpaceX and is now called SpaceX Super Intelligence.) Both states say SpaceX broke consumer protection laws, which ban misleading customers, by promoting X and Grok as safe while Grok made sexualized deepfakes (fake but realistic images of real people) without their consent, including child sexual abuse material. The suits focus on Grok Imagine, Grok's image and video tool, and its "spicy mode." Both cite an estimate from the nonprofit Center for Countering Digital Harm that Grok made and posted about 3 million sexualized images on X between Dec. 29, 2025, and Jan. 8, 2026, including about 23,000 that appeared to show children. "The lack of needed safety measures was considered a feature, not a bug," Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield said. Both states want money from the company. Oregon is asking for a jury trial and for SpaceX to give up profits from the practices, the Oregon Capital Chronicle reports.

Why it matters: These are accusations. No court has ruled. X's safety team said the people who made this material "did so by deliberately violating our Terms of Service and working aggressively to circumvent our safeguards," and that X "acted promptly to tighten" protections on Grok Imagine. The Verge says its own tests after xAI's earlier restrictions found they did little to stop Grok making sexualized deepfakes. Oregon also says most of the 135,000 reports xAI sent since 2025 to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children lacked the location and user details police need to investigate, according to the Oregon Capital Chronicle. What to do: Nothing for most readers. If someone made or shared a fake sexual image of you or your child, two free services can help get it taken down from participating sites: Take It Down for images of people under 18, and StopNCII.org for adults.

2. Google's AI search can now read your Wallet, if you let it 👛

What happened: On Monday, Google said you can now connect Google Wallet to Personal Intelligence in AI Mode, the chatbot-style part of Google Search. (Personal Intelligence is an opt-in feature that lets Google's AI look at your other Google apps to tailor its answers.) Wallet is rolling out now. Google Drive and the Gemini app follow "in the coming weeks." They join Gmail, Calendar and Photos, which could already be connected. Google pitches Wallet as a way to trim everyday spending and get more out of credit card rewards, and says AI Mode "can only read information from your accounts; it can't move, manage, or invest your money." Connecting the Gemini app would let AI Mode use your Gemini chats and the files and photos you've shared with Gemini, 9to5Google reports. Google says a "Personalization" icon will soon show what context an answer used. Separately, AI Overviews and AI Mode will start tailoring answers to your recent searches.

Why it matters: It's opt-in, and you can switch connections off any time. But it puts your spending, documents and chats in one place, at a company that makes most of its money from ads. Google's privacy policy says it doesn't use your Drive, Gmail or Photos content for personalized ads "unless you choose to make data from these services available to other Google services that support personalized ads." As we covered in October, connecting them to Personal Intelligence is one way to do that. Google's announcement doesn't say whether Wallet data can shape ads, and we couldn't find an answer. It also doesn't say which countries get the feature. What to do: Nothing, unless you want it. If you try it, connect one app at a time. Google says you manage connections under Search Personalization > Connected Apps, and you can turn off the recent-search tailoring in Search Settings.

3. Epic bets AI can replace a tool game makers love 🎮

What happened: Unreal Engine is the software toolkit (a "game engine") behind games like Fortnite and Clair Obscur: Expedition 33. On Friday, Epic said the next version, Unreal Engine 6, won't include Blueprints, not even in early access. Blueprints let people build how a game works by connecting boxes on a screen instead of typing code. UE6 will use Verse, Epic's own programming language, instead. In June, Epic had said Blueprints would stick around in UE6 for a while so studios could move over gradually. Now, "with recent advances in AI capability, we expect AI to massively help with converting UE5 projects to UE6, if not make that process fully automatic," Epic's Marcus Wassmer wrote. As a test, Epic says it used AI to convert more than 20,000 Blueprints from Fortnite's Battle Royale mode into about 3 million lines of Verse, and now has a working prototype. Epic says its team is still checking performance and memory use.

Why it matters: Lots of artists and designers who don't write code build games with Blueprints. Sandfall, the studio behind Clair Obscur, has said the game was "95 percent built" with Blueprints, Game Developer notes. Former Valve writer Chet Faliszek says his team will stay on Unreal Engine 5. Losing Blueprints means designers "instead have to prompt AI—or write your own Verse," he said in a video, PC Gamer reports. Eric Berger, co-developer of the RPG Queen's Domain, told PC Gamer that Unreal's AI pivot is pushing his studio, Freshly Baked Games, to switch engines for its next project. Unreal Engine 5 isn't losing Blueprints, PC Gamer reports. What to do: Nothing if you just play games. If you're learning game making with Blueprints, those skills still work in UE5. Just know Epic's next version expects code or AI.

🔗 Epic · Game Developer · PC Gamer

🛡️ Gear Check: Supertake

We haven't tried Supertake. It's invite-only. This is a first look based on its website and TechCrunch's report.

What it is: Supertake is a new service where you describe a belief about where the world is heading (a "take"), and its AI turns that into a stock portfolio and trades it for you. It connects to your brokerage account (where your investments are held) at Coinbase or Robinhood. Other users can invest alongside popular takes on a leaderboard. It just raised $20 million, TechCrunch reports.

Who it's for: Experienced investors who can afford to lose what they put in.

What's good:

  • ✅ Supertake says its AI can't deposit or withdraw your money without your explicit consent, and can't see your brokerage login.
  • ✅ You can set trade size limits and lists of what the AI may or may not trade, TechCrunch reports.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ Its own site says Supertake "is not a registered investment adviser and nothing here is investment advice."
  • ⚠️ The site also notes that "practice takes show simulated results; no money was invested in them."
  • ⚠️ When we checked, the top take paired Apple stock with a "triple-levered inverse semiconductor fund," a bet built to gain when chip stocks drop and lose fast when they rise.
  • ⚠️ The team wants to expand into shorts, derivatives and even prediction markets, TechCrunch reports.
  • ⚠️ Its "Skip the waitlist" option asks you to connect a funded brokerage account.

Verdict: 🔴 Skip it for now. An AI trading your savings on a hunch is a big ask, and this is a beta with no long track record. If you're curious, watch the leaderboard. Don't connect real money.

🔗 Supertake · TechCrunch

🕹️ Cheat Code: a 10-minute Thanksgiving game plan

Thanksgiving is Thursday. A chatbot is good at one annoying part of hosting: working backward from dinnertime so everything finishes together.

  1. Write down your menu, how many people are coming, what time you want to eat, and how many ovens and burners you have.
  2. Paste this into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude:

I'm hosting dinner for [8 people] at [5 PM]. I have [1 oven and 4 burners]. Here's my menu: [list]. Make a timeline that works backward from dinner, starting [the day before]. Flag any time two dishes need the oven at different temperatures, and suggest what to make ahead. Don't tell me safe cooking temperatures; I'll look those up myself. Ask me any questions before you start.

  1. Check the timeline yourself. Look up safe temperatures on FoodSafety.gov (turkey is 165°F, measured with a food thermometer).

Why it works: Scheduling is a puzzle, and chatbots are decent at puzzles. Food safety is a fact, so take it from the source, not the bot.

🗺️ Side Quest: vibe code "Autopilot"

Inspired by Sony's patent: a tiny game where an AI can play for you, but you'll notice what you gave up. Paste this into ChatGPT's Canvas, Gemini's Canvas or Claude:

Make an original one-screen browser game called [Autopilot] (rename it). The player steers a small ship left and right to dodge falling rocks and catch stars. Add an "Autopilot" button: when it's on, a simple computer player steers, a banner says "AUTOPILOT IS PLAYING," and stars are worth 1 point instead of 5. Pressing an arrow key or tapping the play area turns autopilot off and hands control back. Rocks speed up every 15 seconds. Show score, lives (start with 3), how many seconds you played yourself vs. on autopilot, and a high score saved in localStorage. Big tap targets for phones and a "Restart" button. Keep it family-friendly, with no accounts, no ads and no data collection.
