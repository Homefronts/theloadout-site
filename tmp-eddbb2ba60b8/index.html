1. A mathematician says Claude helped him prove the Riemann hypothesis 🧮

What happened: On Friday, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, posted a four-page note on his website. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote. (The Riemann hypothesis, first posed in 1859, is a prediction about how prime numbers, the numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, are spread out. Many mathematicians call it the most important unsolved problem in the field.) The BBC reports he worked on it over about six weeks, using a non-public version of Claude. Instead of a normal paper, he posted the proof in Lean, a programming language that lets a computer check every step of a proof. It runs to 17 million lines of code. A full written-out version, he notes, hit more than 250,000 pages. On Saturday, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, the groups behind Lean and its shared library of checked math, said they have "full confidence" the proof is correct. Dozens of people compiled it, and several independent checking programs accepted it. "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero," MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland told Scientific American.

Why it matters: A computer confirming every step isn't the same as people understanding why it works. Terence Tao, one of the world's best-known mathematicians, said that "digestion" of the proof is still some way off, the BBC reports. Fields Medal winner James Maynard told Nature: "If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics." Not everyone liked how it came out. "What he released is garbled," number theorist Andrew Granville told Nature. He also tied the timing to a drop in Anthropic's stock price; the company started trading on the stock market the day before. Alpöge wrote that the "obvious cynical view" about the timing "is false." Hours after Alpöge's post, Stanford's Ravi Vakil posted a separate, human-written proof attempt by Stanford mathematician Alessandro Masullo. Brian Conrad, who has been advising Masullo, told Scientific American it's "undergoing major revision." The Clay Mathematics Institute offers $1 million for a solution, but only after it's published and survives at least two years of scrutiny, The Verge notes. Anthropic hadn't commented as of The Verge's report. What to do: Nothing. When you see posts saying "AI solved math," the accurate version is: computers have checked it, and people still need to understand it.

2. South Korea's education minister quit after an AI cheating scare 📝

What happened: South Korea's Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned Sunday, and President Lee Jae Myung accepted. It started on November 15, when Han Won-seok, the top scorer on this year's certified tax accountant exam, admitted he'd cheated. "I used a microcamera to photograph the test questions and sent them to an AI, then checked my answers on my smartphone," he said in a video, the BBC reports. He'd gotten 159 of 160 questions right in the first round. That came days before the Suneung, the national college entrance exam about 500,000 students took Thursday. The ministry rushed out new rules: pencils only, stickers over clothing buttons to block hidden cameras, about 8,400 extra staff, and three nationwide emergency alerts during the exam to catch phones that hadn't been handed in. Those alerts blared on phones across the country, even ones on silent. The ministry said it found 111 suspected cheating cases involving electronic devices.

Why it matters: "As the minister in charge, I believe I cannot escape criticism that we failed to prepare in advance," Choi said. Before the exam, a lawmaker asked whether he was 100% confident AI cheating could be detected. The 72-year-old replied, "I don't think I could, because of my age," Yonhap reports. (His ministry later said he meant it's hard for proctors to spot hidden devices, Aju Press reports.) Students and teachers said the last-minute changes added stress. One student told Yonhap he wore contact lenses instead of his usual glasses to avoid raising suspicion, the BBC reports. A tiny camera plus a chatbot can beat a paper test, and so far the answer has been more policing. Officials say they'll analyze Wi-Fi and Bluetooth signals recorded at test sites and refer suspicious cases to investigators. What to do: Nothing for most readers. If you've got a big test coming up, check what's allowed in the room ahead of time, glasses included.

3. OpenAI's chief scientist wants an industry speed limit for AI 🚦

What happened: On Sunday, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published a blog post proposing that AI companies agree on "common limits" they won't cross "until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone." To measure progress, he suggests a score called perplexity. (Perplexity measures how well an AI predicts the next bit of text; lower means better. It has nothing to do with the AI company named Perplexity.) OpenAI also published its own internal scores. Once its models got below about 0.8, Pachocki wrote, "we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics." He also warned that AI could help drive big advances in how AI itself is trained "as soon as 2027," and that safety and monitoring work probably wouldn't keep pace. CEO Sam Altman called the post "the most important thing OpenAI has published in awhile." TechCrunch notes Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei recently called for "limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress" too.

Why it matters: Nothing here is binding. The post doesn't even name a number for the first limit; it asks other companies to help pick one. Pachocki lists the weak spots himself: companies test on different data, so scores are hard to compare, and perplexity doesn't capture gains from reasoning models (AIs that work through a problem step by step before answering). OpenAI's numbers also come from tests on its own internal code. We couldn't find any other major AI company publicly signing on as of Monday morning. What to do: Nothing. Watch whether rivals agree to an actual number, and who would check that anyone sticks to it.

🛡️ Gear Check: Signal's AI screen block

We haven't tried this ourselves. This is a first look based on Signal's announcement and help page.

What it is: Signal, the encrypted messaging app, now blocks screenshots inside the app on iPhones running iOS 27, to stop Apple's Siri AI from reading your chats off the screen. On Android, it turns on a setting called Screen security automatically when it detects an AI assistant like Google's Gemini trying to read the screen. Signal President Meredith Whittaker wrote that AI agents built into phone software are "undermining Signal's ability to independently guarantee privacy." It follows the Muse story we covered Friday.

Who it's for: Anyone who uses Signal, and the people they message.

What's good:

  • ✅ It's on by default on iPhone (Signal 8.37 or later). Nothing to set up.
  • ✅ An "Off for 10 Minutes" option on iPhone lets you grab a quick screenshot, then turns protection back on.
  • ✅ It also hides your chats from the preview when you switch between apps.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ While it's on, you can't screenshot your own chats, like a receipt someone sent you.
  • ⚠️ Signal calls it "incomplete." It doesn't stop AI from reaching data other ways, like files already on your phone.
  • ⚠️ It doesn't work on Mac. Signal suggests adding Signal to Excluded Apps under System Settings > Siri on macOS 27.
  • ⚠️ On Android (Signal 8.35.1 or later), it only kicks in on phones where Signal can detect the AI access.

Verdict: 🟢 Leave it on. Update Signal, then check Settings > Privacy (iPhone: Block Siri AI; Android: App security > Screen security).

🕹️ Cheat Code: the 10-minute "explain it three ways" ladder

Story #1 is a good example of news that's hard to follow. A chatbot can help, as long as you keep it from filling gaps with made-up stuff:

  1. Copy the article you want to understand and paste it into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude.
  2. Paste this prompt:

Explain the main idea of this article three ways: for a 10-year-old, for a high school student, and for a college student. Use only what's in the article. If you add outside background, label it "background." Then ask me 3 quiz questions, one at a time, and wait for my answers.

  1. Take the quiz. Then pick one fact the chatbot gave you and find it in the original article.

Why it works: Three levels show you where your understanding drops off. The "background" label makes it easier to spot anything the chatbot added, which is where mistakes tend to sneak in.

🗺️ Side Quest: vibe code "Prime Time"

Inspired by story #1: a quick number game about the primes behind the Riemann hypothesis. Paste this into ChatGPT's Canvas, Gemini's Canvas or Claude:

Make an original one-screen browser game called [Prime Time] (rename it). Numbers from 2 to 100 float up from the bottom of the screen in bubbles. Tap a bubble only if the number is prime (divisible only by 1 and itself). Right taps score points and build a streak bonus. Tapping a non-prime costs a life and briefly shows why, like "21 = 3 × 7." Letting a prime float away also costs a life. Start with 3 lives. Bubbles speed up every 20 seconds. Show score, lives, streak and a high score saved in localStorage. Big tap targets for phones and a "Restart" button. Keep it family-friendly, with no accounts, no ads and no data collection.
