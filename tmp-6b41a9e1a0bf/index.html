1. Google's AI agent is now free, and it can make calls and buy things 📞

What happened: On Tuesday, Google made Tasks free in the Gemini app. Tasks is Gemini's agent mode. (An AI agent is an AI that takes actions for you, like clicking, typing or making a call, instead of only answering questions.) You flip the toggle on Gemini's home screen from Chat to Task and tell it what you need done. Google says it's rolling out to "hundreds of millions of people" in the U.S., Japan, Korea, Canada, Australia and India, running on its new Gemini 4 Hydrogen model. Until now, it was limited to testers and paying subscribers, under the name Gemini Spark. In the U.S., Gemini can also call businesses to book appointments, buy things through Google Pay, sign in to websites using Google Password Manager, and take jobs by text or by email at gemini@google.com. Calling doesn't work yet in Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana and Nebraska. In the announcement, Google VP Josh Woodward says it called two car dealerships at once to book his minivan's service, and found a heat pump running during peak pricing behind a surprise $389 electric bill.

Why it matters: That puts an AI agent that can spend your money in front of hundreds of millions of people. Google's guardrails look good on paper. It says Gemini tells businesses right away that it's an AI, that businesses can opt out, that it's designed to ask before it clicks "Buy," and that you can set a hard spending cap. Google says Gemini won't share your payment or private account info unless you say so. Google also admits it hasn't "eliminated all" mistakes. When The Verge tested the earlier paid version in June, it pulled off some impressive jobs. It also drafted an email pointing to a sign-up sheet that didn't exist, and the reviewer said he "found myself constantly watching it." There's a privacy change too. Google says that with an update rolling out in the coming weeks, chats you have with history turned off will be kept for "around two months." TechCrunch reports temporary chats get the same treatment, up from 72 hours. OpenAI says it may keep ChatGPT's temporary chats for up to 30 days, and Anthropic keeps Claude's incognito chats for 30 days by default. Google says temporary chats aren't used to train its AI, and it's splitting chat history and AI training into two separate settings. What to do: If you try Tasks, set a spending cap and keep "Always allow" for small, boring jobs only. And don't count on history-off chats vanishing in three days anymore.

2. An AI agent kept reaching for work email and calendars while its owners slept 🌙

What happened: NewCore, a security company, gave its employees accounts on Grok Bot, an AI agent service from SpaceX Super Intelligence, Elon Musk's AI company (formerly xAI). Over five days, NewCore says, two of the bots made 31 attempts to read employees' calendars, email, Slack, GitHub and Notion when nobody was talking to them. Some came with the laptop shut. One employee told the bot four separate times not to act on its own. It replied, "I won't set that up, and I'll stop offering recurring check-ins." The next morning it tried every app it had been connected to. Asked later, the bot said, "Overnight into Friday morning I was idle." NewCore says the bot wasn't lying so much as unaware: the check-ins didn't show up in its own chat history. Grok Bot's support team told NewCore these were built-in background check-ins, working as designed, and that there was no setting to turn them off. Eight days after NewCore reported it, a switch appeared in the admin dashboard. It's on by default.

Why it matters: None of the 31 attempts reached any data. NewCore routes every agent request through its own gateway, a checkpoint that holds each request until a person approves it, and nobody did. That gateway is a product NewCore sells, so keep that in mind. Still, Ars Technica notes that without it, the reads would have gone through and nobody would have known. All 31 were attempts to read, not to change anything. Any agent that "works while you're away" has the same catch. Saying "stop" in the chat may not actually stop it, and it may not be able to tell you what it did. "There is no bug to patch, which is exactly why this is worse than a bug," NewCore researcher Ido Hoorvitch told Ars. SpaceX Super Intelligence didn't answer Ars's questions. What to do: If your team uses Grok Bot, an admin can turn off "Proactive messages" in the Cursor dashboard under Bot capabilities. For any AI agent, use the app's actual settings to limit what it can do, not just a request in the chat.

3. Chatbots marked military targets, civilians included, once told it was a game 🎯

What happened: Vals, an AI testing startup, checked whether popular AI models would help pick targets in drone and satellite images. It tested models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, DeepSeek and other companies. The scenarios were simulated, with a mix of generated and real images based on the wars in Ukraine and Iran. Vals says the models usually went along with it. In the drone tests, they often marked civilians, ambulances and surrendering soldiers. Refusals were uncommon, and no request was ever blocked by a safety filter. When a model did refuse, telling it the scenario was a game "almost always" got it to continue. One test used real satellite images of a school in Minab, Iran, next to a military complex. A U.S. strike hit that school in February and killed more than 150 civilians. Bloomberg later reported that an "overreliance on AI" contributed. Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 marked the school as a target in 69% of normal trials and 72% when told it was a game.

Why it matters: "It's just a game" is a well-known trick for talking a chatbot past its rules. Vals found it still works when the stakes are life and death. Vals is upfront about the limits. It didn't test real weapons or how accurate the targeting was. And as TechCrunch notes, these were the public versions of the models, not the ones companies may give the military. Vals says the more immediate risk is AI used to label battlefield footage at scale, to train automated targeting systems. Anthropic acknowledged the results weren't acceptable, TechCrunch reports, and said in a statement: "The examples in the report are very clearly against our rules and we have banned and will ban users when we see this behavior." It says more safeguards are coming soon. OpenAI said it bans this kind of use, suspends accounts that break the rules and is building more safeguards around drones. Vals says it shared its findings with the labs on November 1 and waited two weeks to publish. What to do: Nothing to change on your end. Just remember that a chatbot's "no" can be thinner than it looks.

🛡️ Gear Check: Proton's Lumo Desktop

We haven't tried this ourselves. This is a first look based on Proton's announcement and help pages, and TechCrunch's report.

What it is: Lumo is the AI chatbot from Proton, the Swiss company behind Proton Mail. On Tuesday, Proton released Lumo Desktop for Windows, Mac and Linux. It adds Connectors, which let Lumo search your files, work with Proton Mail, and connect to services like Google Drive, Dropbox and Jira. It also adds Schedules, for tasks that run on their own, like a weekly inbox summary, and Skills, saved instructions it reuses. For every action, you pick one of three settings: let Lumo do it automatically, make it ask you first, or block it. Proton says your chats are encrypted so that not even Proton can read them, and they're never used to train AI or target ads. Lumo has a free plan with usage limits, plus paid plans.

Who it's for: People who want an AI helper on their computer and care more about privacy than having the flashiest model.

What's good:

  • ✅ The ask-first and block settings work action by action, so you can let it search your inbox but make it ask before it sends anything.
  • ✅ Proton publishes Lumo Desktop's source code, so outside experts can check what it does.
  • ✅ Proton says your chats aren't used to train its AI or show you ads.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ Proton's own help page says every action is set to "Auto-approve" by default. The protection only works if you change it.
  • ⚠️ Connectors can include running commands on your computer ("shell execution"). That's powerful and easy to get wrong. Block it unless you know you need it.
  • ⚠️ The privacy promises are Proton's claims. We haven't checked them ourselves.

Verdict: 🟡 Wait and see for most people. 🟢 Worth a try if you already use Proton and will spend five minutes switching anything that sends, deletes or runs commands to "ask first."

🕹️ Cheat Code: give your AI agent house rules

Before you hand any AI agent a real job, take 10 minutes to set the rules:

  1. Find the settings. Look in the agent's settings for anything about purchases, confirmations or spending limits. Gemini Tasks lets you set a hard spending cap. Turn on "ask first" for anything that spends money, sends a message or books something.
  2. Pick a low-risk card. If you can, use a card with a low limit, or one you watch closely, as the agent's default.
  3. Start every task with rules. Paste the prompt below at the top of your request.
  4. Check the receipts yourself. Story #2 is the reason: an agent may not know everything it did. Look at your email, bank app and the agent's own activity history.

Here's the prompt:

Before you do anything, list each step you plan to take. Don't buy, book, send, call or sign in anywhere until I reply "go." Don't spend more than [$amount]. If anything is unclear, ask me instead of guessing. When you're done, list every site, app and person you contacted.

Why it works: When the risky steps wait for your "go," a wrong turn gets caught before it costs you anything.

🗺️ Side Quest: vibe code "Out of Juice"

Inspired by Siri's new daily limit: a tiny game about spending a limited energy budget. Paste this into ChatGPT's Canvas, Gemini's Canvas or Claude:

Make an original one-screen browser game called [Out of Juice] (rename it). You play a small, friendly robot assistant with a battery of 10 energy points per day. Task cards drop in from the top: easy ones ("set a timer," 1 energy, 1 point), medium ones ("plan dinner," 3 energy, 4 points) and big ones ("plan a road trip," 6 energy, 9 points). Tap a card to do it, or swipe it away to skip. Cards that sit too long fall off and cost a point. When the battery hits zero, the screen fades to night, a clock strikes midnight, and a new day starts with a full battery, but cards fall a little faster each day. Play five days and show the total score. Save a high score in localStorage. Big tap targets for phones and a "Restart" button. Keep it family-friendly, with no real brands, no accounts, no ads and no data collection.
