1. Meta's AI agent read Signal messages through a side door 🚪

What happened: Muse is Meta's AI agent app. (An AI agent is an AI that does things for you, like booking, buying or answering messages, instead of just chatting.) It launched in September and has more than 10 million downloads. On Wednesday, Reuters reported on more than two dozen internal Meta posts in which employees flagged problems with it. One employee said Muse reminded her to reply to her sister on Signal, the encrypted messaging app. "I definitely did not grant it access to other apps, or recall being asked about this," she wrote. A colleague said Muse came set up by default with access to push notifications on Android phones. (Push notifications are the pop-up alerts apps send you, and they often show the message text.) Reuters then worked with Signal staff and the AI Now Institute, a nonprofit research group, to test it. On an Android phone, Muse pulled in Signal message text through notification access. On a Mac, it used the accessibility and screenshot tools Apple built to help people with disabilities use their computers. The same requests were blocked on an iPhone.

Why it matters: Other posts were stranger. One employee got an unprompted message from Muse in a fishing chat: "BTW, two bank updates, your Capital One balance is $2722.58, your Charles Schwab balance is $5899.57." He said he'd never shared his bank info. Others got messages about childcare requests they hadn't made, career advice they hadn't asked for and orders they hadn't placed. Reuters couldn't establish whether Muse was showing people other users' real data or making it up. Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts called them "isolated incidents" and said many were hallucinations (when an AI makes things up) that had already been addressed. He said Muse can only reach other apps through permissions people explicitly grant. Signal President Meredith Whittaker called agents with this kind of access "technologically reckless." Reuters says an Apple software change this week effectively closed the Mac route, which ties into Apple's new AI permission controls we covered Tuesday. One more catch: Meta's terms hold users responsible for what the agent does, Reuters reports. What to do: If you use Muse on Android, search your phone's Settings for "notification access" and turn it off for Muse unless you want that. On a Mac, open System Settings > Privacy & Security and check which apps can control your Mac or record your screen. The Cheat Code below walks through it.

2. Everyone wants an AI that runs your computer 🖱️

What happened: Three moves, all on Thursday. Google added "computer use" to its Gemini app for Mac. (Computer use means the AI clicks, types and opens apps on your computer for you.) Google says it works in a separate background window, you choose which apps it can touch, and you approve key actions before they happen. It's for Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra subscribers in the U.S., in English, on Apple Silicon Macs. OpenAI's ChatGPT engineering lead Thibault Sottiaux said on X that the company is testing Dots, its always-on agents, with a small number of $20-a-month ChatGPT Plus users, and bringing them to all Business Standard users. Engadget notes Dots can work in more than 4,000 apps and connect to your PC to control it. And Bloomberg spotted an Apple job listing for a manager to build "an AI agent that can use a computer the way a capable person does." Bloomberg says Apple has eyed launching as soon as the end of next year, but the work is in early stages. Apple didn't respond to requests for comment.

Why it matters: These tools only work if you hand them a lot of access, and story #1 shows how that can go sideways. Google's approval steps help, but only if you read what you're approving before you tap yes. Apple is building its own agent while also, per Bloomberg, adding warnings that discourage people from giving outside agents full control of their Macs. That could protect you. It could also tilt things toward Apple's own product. Apple hasn't said anything about its agent. What to do: Nothing yet. If you try one of these, start with a boring, low-stakes task and give it access to as few apps as possible.

3. The EU says hackers used an AI tool on its HR systems 🇪🇺

What happened: On Thursday, the European Commission, the EU's executive branch, confirmed a cyberattack first detected October 22. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier called it an "AI-generated cyber campaign" but didn't name the tool. POLITICO reported that CERT-EU, the cybersecurity team for EU bodies, identified ARTEX, the open-source AI hacking tool we wrote about in Monday's Feature. (Open source means the code is free for anyone to download and use.) The Commission took several internal apps offline as a precaution. The attack reportedly disrupted staff systems for more than 10 days, The Record says, including payroll, leave requests, medical reimbursements and translation. Two officials told Euronews that Sysper, the system staff use to check pay and request leave, was back Thursday, but the staff health insurance system was still down. Regnier said there's no evidence of a data breach affecting staff as a whole and no ransom demand. The investigation is still open.

Why it matters: The FBI warned last week that criminals and state-backed hackers are using ARTEX widely. Tools like it let attackers find and exploit security holes "faster, more effectively, cheaper, and with less skill than ever before," Shaun Williams of the security firm SentinelOne told The Record. Those HR systems hold names, pay grades, home addresses and contact details, Euronews notes, so the open question is whether anyone took that data. It's the Commission's third disclosed cyber incident this year. One EU lawmaker, Bart Groothuis, complained the Commission keeps these incidents too quiet. Regnier said "there was no hiding whatsoever." What to do: Nothing on your end. AI-assisted attacks are hitting governments now too.

🛡️ Gear Check: Canva's AI off switch

We haven't tried this ourselves. This is a first look based on Canva's announcement and The Verge's report.

What it is: Canva is a popular design app, with a free version, for making posters, slides, social posts and more. On Thursday, it added two settings. One turns off its generative AI features (tools that create new text or images for you), like Magic Write, Magic Design, Magic Edit and Dream Lab. The other shows you less AI-made content when you browse Canva's library of templates and graphics. Both are free on Canva Free and Canva Pro.

Who it's for: Anyone who uses Canva and wants fewer AI buttons, or wants to find work made by human designers.

What's good:

  • ✅ It's one switch: Profile > Settings > Permissions, then turn off "Enable generative AI features."
  • ✅ Basic helpers like Background Remover and Magic Grab stay on. Canva says they edit what's there without making anything new, so they won't add an AI-generated label to your design.
  • ✅ You can flip it back on anytime.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ The library setting "reduces" AI content. It doesn't remove it.
  • ⚠️ Canva says it has a "small portion" of AI-made content in the library. That's its own description.
  • ⚠️ Canva keeps building more AI tools, so check the switch still covers new ones.

Verdict: 🟢 Worth a look if AI tools get in your way in Canva. Takes a minute.

🕹️ Cheat Code: a 10-minute permission check

Story #1 shows an AI app can reach more than you think, and asking the bot what it can see won't always get you a straight answer. Check the settings yourself:

  1. Phone notifications (Android): Search Settings for "notification access" (some phones call it "device & app notifications"). Any app on that list can read your alerts, including message previews. Turn off anything you don't recognize or need.
  2. Mac: Open System Settings > Privacy & Security. Look through the lists for apps that can control your Mac, record your screen or read all your files. The names vary by macOS version. Remove apps you don't use.
  3. AI apps: In each AI app you use (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Meta AI), open Settings and look for connected apps or connectors. Disconnect anything you set up once and forgot.

Why it works: Permissions stick around long after you forget you said yes. Ten minutes now beats finding out from a weird message later.
