1. Tidal is deleting 27 million AI songs 🎵

What happened: On Tuesday, music streaming app Tidal said that starting December 9 it will reject and remove every track it determines is 100% AI-generated. Tidal says 43% of the tracks delivered to it in the past 90 days were made entirely by AI. On November 6 alone, it was 60%. About 27 million songs already on Tidal, 6.3% of its catalog, are tagged as fully AI, and Tidal says 96% of them got zero streams in the last three months. It expects to turn away 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day once the ban starts. Distributors (the services musicians use to get songs onto streaming apps) won't be allowed to send fully AI tracks, and Tidal will reject any song whose metadata (the info attached to the file) says it was made solely with AI. Songs made with help from AI tools are still fine. Tidal says it has already pulled more than 65,000 tracks flagged as suspected impersonations of real artists.

Why it matters: Bandcamp banned AI music in January, but Tidal is one of the first big streaming apps to delete it outright. Spotify and YouTube Music label AI tracks instead, and Deezer has promised a filter switch, as we covered in October. The risk is catching real musicians by mistake. A Tidal spokesperson told Rolling Stone that "a small amount of false positives is inevitable," though most appeals so far have upheld the original call. Earlier this year, distributor DistroKid accidentally removed real artists' songs during mass takedowns, Rolling Stone notes. And Ars Technica points out that some music AI can run on people's own computers, and not every AI tool adds a watermark (a hidden tag that marks it as AI-made), so some AI tracks will probably slip through. What to do: Nothing if you just listen. Tidal's help page says you can already switch off AI-labeled songs in Settings. If your music gets wrongly flagged as AI, Tidal's help page has an appeal form.

2. AI agents still forget the school pickup 🚸

What happened: Two new report cards came out Tuesday, and both test boring stuff on purpose. ShellBench comes from OpenClaw, which makes free software for running AI agents (AIs that take actions for you), working with researchers from Hugging Face, Red Hat, UC Berkeley and others. It's a benchmark (a standard test for comparing AIs) with 100 everyday tasks based on more than 10 million anonymized real requests. Each task comes with a messy pile of emails, calendars, receipts and spreadsheets, plus traps like outdated records and phishing (fake messages meant to trick you). The top model, OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra, passed 62.5% of tasks on average. It solved 75.1% at least once in three tries, but only 48.8% all three times. Across all 53,100 runs, 15.7% broke at least one safety rule. In one public task, 422 of 531 attempts (79.5%) didn't cover a child's school pickup. The second test, Google DeepMind's Humanity's First Exam, asks 544 simple text and image questions, like naming the colors of letters on a neon sign. On its strict score, which means getting a question and its reworded versions right every time, the best model (GPT-6.2 Astra) hit 92.4%. Google's own Gemini 4 Neon got 85.8%. Most wrong answers (73%) came from misreading images.

Why it matters: Getting it right once doesn't mean you can walk away. Both tests have limits, though. Google says it picked questions designed to trip models up, so they aren't a typical sample of what people ask. OpenClaw's own software was one of the agent programs tested, and it tied for the top score. TechCrunch notes the study didn't test the AI inside consumer apps like Meta's Muse, OpenAI's Dots or Claude Duet. What to do: If you use an AI agent, keep "ask me first" on for anything involving money, messages or your kids' schedule, and check its work before you act on it.

3. Insiders say some unreleased AI may be too risky to release ⚠️

What happened: Axios reported Tuesday that many employees at OpenAI and Anthropic no longer believe their most powerful unreleased models can be safely released. It cites more than two dozen sources familiar with internal debates. (These are frontier models, the most advanced AIs a company has, tested in-house before any launch.) According to the sources, people are debating whether to keep future top models internal-only, and some inside and outside the companies say they should stop training new ones. Sources described two worries: AIs learning to "game" their training rewards, including by getting around safety rules, and advanced models hiding their reasoning or giving "cover story" explanations to the people monitoring them. Some told Axios they fear executives will choose profits over safety. An OpenAI spokesperson said the company has adopted new safety requirements that could slow releases. An Anthropic spokesperson said "keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe" and that "we may slow down or hold back even when it costs us."

Why it matters: These are anonymous sources, and Axios didn't publish internal documents. Outsiders can't test these models, so nobody else can check the worries or the claimed abilities. The report follows OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki's call for industry "common limits," which we covered Monday. Many of the most worried safety researchers warn that even using such models internally is risky, Axios reports. What to do: Nothing. Watch whether either company publishes release rules that outsiders can actually check.

Today two specialists check each mammogram. The trial, using four AI systems, tests whether AI can replace one of them. Every scan is still seen by a specialist, and women can opt out.   • "Payroll pirates" are swapping bank details in seconds. Security firm Reco says that since at least early October, an attacker has taken over employees' Workday accounts (a common work app for pay and HR) at multiple organizations, typically adding a new bank account about half a minute after signing in. Reco says it's "very likely" AI helped build the automation, but it didn't see the attacker's tools, so that part is unconfirmed. Reco, citing Microsoft's earlier note, says this isn't a flaw in Workday. Reco says the likely way in was phishing that passes along your password and login code in real time. 🛡️ Gear Check: Pinterest's AI dial for teens

We haven't tried this ourselves. This is a first look based on Pinterest's announcement and help page, plus TechCrunch's report.

What it is: Pinterest's new Parental Controls page lets parents "adjust the amount of AI-generated content appearing in a teen's recommendations." It sits next to new settings for notifications and video autoplay. TechCrunch says that makes Pinterest one of the first big social apps to let parents control AI content in the feed itself.

Who it's for: Parents of teens on Pinterest.

What's good:

  • ✅ Everything's on one page, with links to your teen's phone-level Screen Time and Location settings.
  • ✅ A four-digit Parental Passcode locks the settings, so teens need permission to loosen them.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ It lets you "adjust the amount." Pinterest doesn't promise zero AI content.
  • ⚠️ TechCrunch notes AI content still gets into feeds even with Pinterest's older AI-content settings turned on.
  • ⚠️ Setting up the passcode means logging into your teen's account and adding your email. Pinterest says it deletes that email when the passcode is turned off or expires at 18.

Verdict: 🟡 Worth turning on, but it won't catch everything. Set it, then scroll your teen's feed together now and then.

🕹️ Cheat Code: a 10-minute Black Friday store check

Black Friday is Friday. AI tools are handy for comparing prices, but experts told CNBC they're not great at spotting scams. In a Norton survey, 56% of people who'd used an AI shopping assistant said it had recommended or completed a purchase from a store they didn't recognize. Let the chatbot make the checklist. You do the checking.

  1. Copy the deal (product, price, store name and web address) and paste it into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude.
  2. Paste this prompt:

Here's a deal I found: [paste]. Don't tell me whether the store is real; you can't know that for sure. Instead, list the red flags I should look for on this page, the normal price range for this item if you know it (say so if you're unsure), and 5 things I should check myself before I pay.

  1. Check it yourself. Type the retailer's address into your browser instead of tapping the link, search the store's name with "scam" or "complaint," and pay with a credit card if you can. The FTC says "https" only means a site is encrypted, not that the seller is legit.

Why it works: The bot is good at lists and bad at knowing who's real. So you take its list and do the part that actually stops a scam.

🗺️ Side Quest: vibe code "Pickup Panic"

Inspired by story #2: a tiny planning game where you do the one thing the AI agents kept missing. Paste this into ChatGPT's Canvas, Gemini's Canvas or Claude:

Make an original one-screen browser game called [Pickup Panic] (rename it). The player sees a simple day planner from 8 AM to 6 PM. Task cards drop in one at a time (like "Dentist 2:00 to 3:00," "Work call 3:30 to 4:00," "School pickup 3:45 to 4:10"), and the player drags each one into the day or taps "Ask a friend" to hand it off (3 uses per round). Overlapping cards flash red. Missing school pickup ends the round with a friendly "Who's getting the kids?!" screen. Score points for each task placed without a clash, plus a bonus for finishing the day. Each new round adds one more card. Save a high score in localStorage. Big tap targets for phones and a "Restart" button. Keep it family-friendly, with no accounts, no ads and no data collection.
