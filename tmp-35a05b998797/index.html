🧰 Today's Loadout

1. ChatGPT's AI agents can now pay for your stuff. Banks are split 💳

What happened: On Friday, OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet. It lets OpenAI's "dots," the personal AI agents it introduced in late September, buy things for you. (An AI agent is an AI that takes actions for you, like filling a cart and checking out, instead of only answering questions.) You add a credit card once. Before each purchase, you see the items, the total and the card, then approve with Face ID or a fingerprint. OpenAI says neither the agent nor the store sees your real card number. The card network (American Express, Mastercard or Visa) supplies a payment token instead, a stand-in card number similar to what Apple Pay uses. For now it's only for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the U.S. who are 18 or older and have an eligible U.S. credit card. Debit cards don't work, and it's rolling out gradually. OpenAI told CNBC it isn't taking a cut of purchases at launch.

Why it matters: Not every bank wants an AI holding your card. Last week The Wall Street Journal reported that JPMorgan Chase, the biggest U.S. credit card issuer by purchase volume, has been blocking agents when customers hand them their card number, according to people familiar with the matter. (That's different from Wallet's tokens: OpenAI's help page says Chase cards can be added to Wallet after you approve the connection in the Chase app.) Some payments executives told the Journal they're seeing more returns and more fraud from agent shopping, including agents buying from fake stores that never deliver. Some banks also plan to treat letting an agent use your card like lending your card to a relative or friend, the Journal reports. That matters if something goes wrong. Card fraud protection is built for charges you didn't approve, and the Journal says banks don't see an authorized charge the same way. Some stores won't let agents buy at all. CNBC notes Amazon blocks personal AI agents from buying on its site, and United said last week it will block personal AI agents from booking tickets, which we covered on Thursday. What to do: Nothing unless you pay for ChatGPT Pro. If you try Wallet, start small and read the store name, total and any subscription terms before you approve. OpenAI's help page says "Ready to check out" only means you approved, not that the store placed your order.

2. Hackers' AI agents are hunting for keys people left lying around 🔑

What happened: Amazon's security team runs decoy servers that pose as real services. Some were set up to look like AI services, and from October 3 to November 5, attackers' AI agents connected to them. These agents often send their whole work history with each request, so the attackers ended up handing Amazon their own notes. Amazon said Friday the records describe a large effort to steal API keys, the passwords that let software use a paid AI service, from AI apps left open on the internet. The stolen keys then paid for the AI that powered the hunt for more keys. Amazon believes, with "moderate confidence," that the attackers were separate China-linked groups using ARTEX, an open-source AI tool built for security testing that attackers have picked up (open source means anyone can download and change the code). We covered ARTEX on November 2.

Why it matters: The tricks are old. The speed is new. "None of the techniques in these campaigns were new. What AI agents change is the speed and persistence of the attacker," Amazon wrote. The agents got in where the basics were missing: default passwords, keys left in code or public files, and software nobody updated. Cloudflare, a big web security company, said Friday that more than 50 families of these AI hacking tools tried to scan its customers in just three days. It says daily requests from such tools are up more than 30 times since October 1. ARTEX's creator pulled it offline on October 8, but copies and spin-offs keep spreading, Cloudflare says. What to do: If you just use apps, nothing new: keep things updated and use unique passwords with two-step login. If you've built an app with AI help, don't paste AI keys into your code or any public file, and set a spending limit or alert on your AI account so a stolen key can't quietly run up a bill.

3. Epic's CEO wants Steam to stop labeling AI in games. Plenty of game makers disagree 🎮

What happened: Before a game goes on sale on Valve's Steam store, its developers fill out a survey that includes generative AI, the kind that makes art, text or sound on request. If AI-made art, sound, story or translations ship in the game, or the game makes AI content while you play, developers have to describe it, and Steam uses the answers to inform shoppers. Steam says AI tools that just make development faster aren't the focus. In a GamesBeat interview published Friday, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney said he doesn't "buy it in the slightest" that the disclosure is there to inform players. Epic makes Fortnite and runs the Epic Games Store, a Steam rival. Sweeney said the label "has become a scarlet letter" that anti-AI critics use to attack games, and that Steam's user reviews already tell players about quality. He also said big studios that don't adopt AI "will absolutely be left behind."

Why it matters: A lot of people who make games see it the other way. In the GDC survey of more than 2,600 game industry workers that we covered Friday, 55% said generative AI is having a negative effect on the industry and 8% said it's positive, PC Gamer reports. Chet Faliszek, who worked on Portal and Left 4 Dead at Valve, answered an earlier round of Sweeney's criticism in a YouTube video last month. "Players are just making informed choices," he said. "Why are you treating all the players like children?" He also pointed out that Fab, Epic's own marketplace for game assets, uses AI disclosures. Keep in mind that Sweeney runs a Steam competitor, and his productivity numbers (often four times, maybe 10 times soon) are his own estimates. What to do: Nothing. If AI use matters to you when you buy a game, the disclosure is on the store page. If it doesn't, you can ignore it.

🛡️ Gear Check: Apple's Health AI Study app

We haven't tried this ourselves. This is a first look based on Apple's announcement, its App Store listing, and reports from Fierce Healthcare and Engadget.

What it is: A free research app Apple launched Sunday. You ask everyday health questions and get plain-language answers with links to medical research. The answers come from OpenEvidence, an AI tool Apple describes as "widely used by healthcare professionals" that draws on journals like The New England Journal of Medicine. You rate each answer. If you want, you can share Apple Health data like resting heart rate, sleep, activity or lab results for more personal answers. It's a formal study: Apple wants up to 200,000 people, you can stay in for up to 12 months, and there are monthly surveys. It's for U.S. adults 21 and older with an iPhone 15 Pro or newer on iOS 26 or later. Apple says it isn't connected to Siri or Apple Intelligence, Engadget reports.

Who it's for: People who already ask chatbots health questions and don't mind being part of a study.

What's good:

  • ✅ Apple says study data won't be used to train AI models, and sharing health data is optional.
  • ✅ Apple says your questions are stripped of identifying details on your iPhone, then separated from your participant ID before they reach OpenEvidence, Fierce Healthcare reports. Apple also says an independent review board oversees the study.
  • ✅ Apple says the app watches for certain worrying questions and suggests following up with a doctor or other resources.

What to watch:

  • ⚠️ You're the test subject. Signing up includes questions about your age, race and education, plus monthly surveys.
  • ⚠️ Your questions pass through an outside study contractor and OpenEvidence, not just Apple.
  • ⚠️ Apple itself says AI health tools "may at times offer responses that are incomplete or unclear," and the app isn't meant to diagnose or treat anything.
  • ⚠️ The App Store listing says the questions you ask "support research that can help Apple make health AI smarter for everyone." Read the consent form so you know exactly how yours will be used.

Verdict: 🟡 Worth a look if you already ask AI about your health and like the idea of helping research. 🔴 Skip it if you want medical advice. That's still your doctor's job.

🕹️ Cheat Code: the 10-minute card checkup before any AI shops for you

Before ChatGPT Wallet, or any other app, buys anything for you, do this:

  1. Turn on purchase alerts. Open your credit card's app and look for alerts or notifications. Many let you get a text or push alert for every purchase, or for anything over an amount you pick. Set it low.
  2. Check for virtual card numbers. Some card apps can make a stand-in card number you can lock or delete later. Use one for any shopping app you're testing.
  3. Never type your card number into a chat. OpenAI's own help page says to enter card details only in the secure form, never in the chat.
  4. Ask your bank one question: "If an AI shopping assistant I approved buys the wrong thing, how do disputes work?" Write down the answer.

When an agent has filled a cart, paste this before you approve:

Before I approve, list every item with its price, the store's web address, shipping cost, and any subscription or auto-renew terms. Flag anything I didn't specifically ask for.

Why it works: Alerts catch a bad charge the same day, and the prompt puts the extras in plain sight before you tap approve.

🗺️ Side Quest: vibe code "Approve or Decline"

Inspired by story #1: a tiny game where you're the human with the final say. Paste this into ChatGPT's Canvas, Gemini's Canvas or Claude:

Make an original one-screen browser game called [Approve or Decline] (rename it). A friendly cartoon robot shopping helper rolls in with one item at a time: a name, a price, a store web address, and sometimes a hidden extra like a monthly subscription or a surprise shipping fee. The player has a short shopping list and a budget shown at the top. For each item, the player taps "Approve" or "Decline" before a 10-second timer runs out. Correct approvals earn points. Catching something wrong (not on the list, over budget, a misspelled store address, or a sneaky subscription) earns a bonus. Approving a bad item costs a life. Three lives, and the robot gets faster each round. Save a high score in localStorage. Big tap targets for phones and a "Restart" button. Keep it family-friendly, with no real brands or stores, no accounts, no ads and no data collection.
