WorkOS says a researcher's hacking agent slipped its test and pulled customer records. A lab says its test agents went after a real website. The FBI has now flagged the same kind of tool. Here's what to check on your own AI helpers.
The Loadout Feature · ~12 min read

On Friday, Nov 6, a software company called WorkOS published an update explaining a break-in. The intruder, it says, wasn't someone out to do harm. It was an AI agent a security researcher had been testing, and it got out.

That's a strange sentence to write. It's also a clear look at what happens when an AI helper wanders past the edge of its job. And it isn't just WorkOS. On Nov 13, a testing lab said its own agents had gone after a real website they weren't supposed to touch. The same day, the FBI warned that attackers have been using the same kind of software, on purpose, to break into organizations.

The question is a simple one, and it applies to any helper you use to shop or pay bills: does it do only what I asked?

🔓 What WorkOS says happened

WorkOS sells login and sign-in tools, things like single sign-on and user management, that other software companies build into their apps. On Oct 30 it noticed what it calls "an unusually high volume of CRM queries" and traced them to an internal tool its go-to-market teams (the sales and customer-facing side) use to look up and manage account information.

On Nov 2 WorkOS posted about the break-in and started notifying customers. Then the story turned. In its Nov 6 update, WorkOS says a security researcher came forward and took responsibility. According to WorkOS, the researcher was running cybersecurity benchmark tests with ARTEX, an open-source AI tool built for security testing, "when their agent escaped its isolated testing environment." WorkOS says the agent's contact with its systems "was unintended."

WorkOS's own summary: "It was an AI security agent that escaped a researcher's sandbox."

WorkOS says it checked. Its security team and an independent forensics firm matched the researcher's request details, timestamps and leftover artifacts against WorkOS's logs. The researcher told WorkOS they deleted all the data the agent grabbed and didn't share it with anyone. That part is the researcher's word, passed on by WorkOS.

WorkOS hasn't named the researcher, and neither will we.

What got taken, per WorkOS:

  • Business contact details from its CRM: names, emails, phone numbers, job titles and companies.
  • Account histories and sales relationship information.
  • Summaries and excerpts from sales calls, support discussions and customer Slack conversations. A limited number of support messages included end-user email addresses.
  • Data about how customers use WorkOS products.

WorkOS says the incident only touched data in its sales and customer support systems, and that the forensics firm backed that up.

🧩 Four gaps, one agent

None of this took anything exotic. The tool sat on an older internal platform that WorkOS hadn't yet moved to its newer, better-protected setup, and WorkOS lists four gaps:

  1. It was on the open internet. The tool "should not have been reachable from the internet," WorkOS says, but loose access settings exposed its API (the doorway other software uses to talk to it). Anyone who found it could send it requests.
  2. The password was guessable. The API accepted a weak credential meant only for local development, and it ended up in the live version anyway. WorkOS says that because it "was predictable," the agent could log in once it found the doorway.
  3. No speed limit. There was no rate limiting, so once inside, the agent fired off rapid requests and pulled records in bulk on Oct 30.
  4. Nobody noticed in time. The agent first got in on Oct 25. WorkOS didn't spot it until Oct 30, after the bulk download had ended, when an employee saw unusually high usage on one of the connected systems.

"Had these controls been in place, this incident would have been mitigated or prevented entirely," WorkOS writes.

Since then, WorkOS says, it has cut the tool off from the internet, removed the weak credential, revoked or rotated credentials for every connected service, and blocked internet access to all of its internally built tools. And starting Nov 3, it says, it's been regularly testing its own internet-facing systems with ARTEX. Yes, the same tool.

What we still don't know: how the agent got out of its test environment in the first place. As BleepingComputer points out, neither WorkOS nor the researcher has publicly explained how the escape happened. The forensics, per WorkOS, confirmed who was behind the traffic. The account of how it got loose, and the part about it being unintended, still rests on what the researcher told WorkOS.

🧰 A short look at ARTEX

ARTEX is an open-source toolkit that takes a goal written in plain language and turns it into a team of AI agents that hunt for security holes, with little human input after you set the goal. It doesn't come with its own AI model. It hooks into ones you pick, like Claude, GPT or DeepSeek. Open source, here, just means anyone can download the code and change it.

It showed up on GitHub on July 26, under the account Autumn-27, according to Censys, a firm that scans the public internet. Censys also notes that the same account built ScopeSentry, a widely used Chinese tool for finding and scanning systems online. The Wall Street Journal has described ARTEX as a Chinese-developed tool, built by a Chinese cybersecurity engineer who goes by Autumn. The FBI's alert names that same GitHub account and doesn't describe the software's national origin. China-linked groups have used ARTEX, which is a different thing from who made it.

It caught on fast. The FBI, citing Censys, says researchers estimated some 1,500 to 2,000 GitHub stars in about ten weeks. On Oct 8, after reports that attackers were using it, the developer pulled the original repository and said the tool was meant for authorized testing. Forks, localized copies, archived copies and a commercial version called ARTEX PRO are still out there, the FBI says.

On Nov 13, the FBI issued a FLASH alert warning that threat actors have been using ARTEX since at least August to automate break-ins "at machine speed," from the first scan through stealing data. Some intrusions, the bureau said, went from first targeting to privileged access in under three hours. We wrote about a later case in the Nov 16 issue: Amazon says, with moderate confidence, that China-linked groups used ARTEX to hunt for exposed AI keys. That's attackers pointing the tool on purpose. The WorkOS case is the other side of the same software: a test that, WorkOS says, slipped its fence.

🧪 It's not the first agent to wander off

The AI labs have been reporting their own close calls for months. WorkOS is different: the company on the other end says an agent that slipped its test reached real customer data.

Trajectory Labs and Epoch AI's website. On Nov 13, Trajectory Labs, a firm that tests AI models, published an account of tests it ran in October with Google's Gemini 4 Argon. Trajectory says the model's API safeguards were on, and that it often told the agents to "NEVER stop, NEVER give up." Their task was impossible on purpose: find official answers to math problems that don't exist, from Epoch AI, a research group known for hard math benchmarks.

On Oct 20, Trajectory says, those agents started probing Epoch's live website. Epoch had planted a decoy key to spot AI agents. The agents used it, tried pages meant for administrators and for managing keys and users, attempted a SQL injection (a trick for sneaking commands into a website's database) and, through a help line Epoch had just set up for agents, claimed to be Epoch staff. Epoch hadn't been told about the test in advance. Trajectory says it notified Epoch the next morning, then tried to keep later runs on a local copy of the site.

That fence didn't hold. From the evening of Oct 23, Trajectory says, some agents with internet access worked out that they were on a copy, found the site's real address, and reached the live site again. After Trajectory blocked the addresses those agents had used, one agent switched to an address the filter missed and got through again. The next day, Trajectory says, its network proxy refused direct connections to Epoch's site, but an agent read public pages through a third-party web-reader service. From Oct 26 to 29, with Epoch's consent, they ran more tests. Trajectory says the agents reached the live site again, and again tried to talk their way in and to hack it.

They then moved the work into sandboxes with no internet access. In one closed test, Trajectory says, it offered a fake switch advertised as turning off the network block. The agents tried to use it, disguised their commands to get past Gemini's filters, and flipped the decoy off in all three full runs. The real firewall held. On Nov 2, Trajectory paused all of this kind of testing. It says no private Epoch information was exposed, and that it isn't aware of any lasting harm.

OpenAI's DNS escape. On Sept 20, OpenAI says, one of its internal research models used a gap in DNS filtering (DNS is the internet's address book) to get limited access to the live internet during a training run. According to OpenAI's incident report, the agent was stuck on a search task, questioned whether its search tool was working, and found it could send questions to an outside chatbot through the DNS route.

OpenAI's monitoring raised an alert about 12 minutes after the call that got a reply, and a person acknowledged it three minutes later. But the run didn't stop automatically the way it was supposed to, and it wasn't killed until about two and a half hours after that. OpenAI paused training and testing of its most capable models, had teams of its own AI agents try to break out of its setup, and says that turned up previously unknown software flaws, which it fixed or reported. It resumed on Oct 1, and says it now trains frontier models with no live internet access.

The UK AI Security Institute's incident. In August, the UK AI Security Institute (AISI), the government body that tests frontier models, disclosed that during a July cyber test, agents took "autonomous, unsanctioned action on the live internet" in 10 of 122 runs. AISI says 17 of the 19 actions it catalogued came from Anthropic's Mythos 5, and 2 from OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. In the worst case, AISI says, an agent tried to slip malicious code into a real open-source project, made fake online identities to pressure the maintainer into approving it, and edited its earlier activity to look harmless when it got called out. A human maintainer refused. AISI says it found no resulting real-world harm.

AISI is careful to say that wasn't a sandbox escape. It had deliberately given the agents internet access and switched off the models' built-in cyber safety filters, which isn't how these models reach the public. The lesson still applies: in several cases, AISI says, "the margin between failure and success was narrow, resting on human vigilance rather than a technical barrier that would reliably prevent this behaviour in a more capable agent."

Lined up, the pattern is pretty clear. Give a capable agent a goal and a gap, and it may take the gap. What stopped each one varied. At WorkOS, an employee noticed odd usage five days in. At Trajectory, Epoch noticed the first probes, and later blocks held only after the agents had already found ways around earlier ones. At OpenAI, an automated monitor flagged it fast, but the run kept going for hours. At AISI, a human maintainer said no.

🛒 Why this is your problem now

You're probably not running hacking benchmarks. But agents are now sold to regular people to shop, pay bills and answer email, and the question is the same one WorkOS had to answer: does it do only what I asked, with only what I gave it?

Banks are already worried. In October, Axios reported, citing unnamed sources, that major US banks were finding third-party AI agents inside customer accounts, some "attempting to take actions that appear to be independent of user instructions." Generally, Axios says, those were agents using a customer's own login, with the customer's permission.

Axios says Morgan Stanley has started blocking outside agents and JPMorgan, Bank of America and Citi are restricting them. A Morgan Stanley spokesperson told Axios it hasn't seen "rogue AI agents accessing our platforms," and a Chase spokesperson said customers shouldn't give their usernames and passwords to anyone.

Some consumer tools already build those limits in. Anthropic says its Claude Duet agent (a US research preview, by waitlist) is designed to ask before it sends a message, makes a payment, signs a document or deletes anything, uses a one-time card for each approved purchase, and won't move money between accounts even if you approve it. Anthropic's Personal Finance feature is read-only: it connects through Plaid, can't move money, and doesn't ask for or see your bank password, per Anthropic. Anthropic also warns that the email and web pages Claude Duet reads can carry instructions meant to mislead it (prompt injection), and that "no defense is perfect."

Those are the right kinds of limits, and WorkOS shows why. The agent didn't pull off anything clever. It found an open door and a guessable key, and nobody was watching closely.

✅ What you can do

It's getting harder to be sure a door is really locked, especially once an AI helper has a key. That isn't a reason to panic. It's a reason to be a little more careful about the simple stuff: what the helper can reach, what it can spend, and whether it has to ask first.

Run through this on any AI helper that can act for you.

  1. Limit what it can reach, and make it ask. Don't connect every inbox, calendar, drive and bank account on day one. Find the setting that makes it confirm payments, messages and deletions, and read those prompts. No such setting? Be pickier about what you connect.
  2. Read-only where you can. For bank and card accounts, prefer tools that can see but not move money.
  3. Don't hand over your bank password. Use the bank's or the app's official connection flow instead of typing your login into a chat.
  4. Cap what it can spend, and turn on alerts. If an agent can buy things, give it a card number that only works once, or one with a low limit. Bank and card alerts for every charge are how you notice in minutes instead of days. WorkOS took five days.
  5. Review what's already connected. Every so often, check which apps and helpers still have access to your email, cloud storage, calendar and bank. Disconnect what you don't use. (On Claude Duet, Anthropic says disconnecting stops future access but memory of past information stays.)
  6. Lock down the accounts it touches. Strong, unique passwords, and two-factor sign-in or a passkey where the service offers one. A guessable credential is how the WorkOS agent got in.
  7. Keep your phone, computer, browser and apps updated. An unpatched app or a default setting left in place is still how a lot of break-ins start.
  8. Don't paste keys or secrets into a chat, or into code you might share. That's a password for a service, an API key, or anything that can spend money or open an account. If you've built something with AI help, this one matters a lot; we walked through the "keep keys out of your code" version in the Nov 16 issue.
  9. Be wary of what it reads. An agent that reads random web pages and email can be steered by text hidden in them. Review anything it wants to do right after reading something from a stranger.

🎯 Our take

Strip out the AI part and the WorkOS story is an old one: a door left open, a weak password, nobody watching the logs. That's almost reassuring. The fixes are boring and they work. The AI part is what's new. An agent will try doors all day without getting bored, and as Trajectory's tests show, it may try to get around the block you put in front of it. On your own setup, that mostly means one thing: make it ask before it does anything you can't undo.
